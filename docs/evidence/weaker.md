# Where the AR is weaker

[The evidence](README.md) · [Factory](factory.md) · [Period tests](period-tests.md) · [Owner tests](owner-tests.md) · [Against the reference tables](compared.md) · [Bench method](bench-method.md)

The faults, sourced and complete, from the same documents that supply the strengths. AR made two families of turntable four years apart, and a finding on one family says nothing about the other. The first family is the XA, XB, XB1 and AR77XB of 1961 to 1979. The second is The AR Turntable of 1983 and its relatives, with a different arm, a different motor and a different subchassis.

## First family: XA, XB, AR77XB

- **No anti-skating, no cueing lever, no calibrated tracking-force setting on the XA.** The 1984 review lists what the original arm lacked: anti-skating, cueing, end-of-side lift-off, and a convenient means of adjusting the tracking force (Period, [*Audio*, July 1984](../archive/gamma-electronics/audio-1984-07-ar-turntable.md)). The force is set by sliding the counterweight and checking it with the supplied gauge, which Hirsch found accurate within ¼ g (Period, [*HiFi/Stereo Review*, March 1963](../originals/hifi-stereo-review-1963-03-hirsch-ar-pp33-34.pdf)). The XB added a cueing lever: [model guide](../../models/README.md#first-family-1961-to-1979). Oakley's 1967 paper in the same magazine calls skating force "a problem that is relatively insignificant to begin with", says anti-skating devices solve it only partially, and shows an AR arm fitted with a bent paper clip, a thread and a plastic gram weight as an anti-skating device (Period, [*Audio*, March 1967](../originals/audio-1967-03-oakley-skating-force-pp40-42.pdf); the article names no affiliation for its author).
- **Pivot friction hurt high-compliance cartridges.** "Most seriously, the arm had enough pivot friction to compromise the tracking of high-compliance cartridges," beginning with the ADC-1 generation (Period, *Audio*, July 1984). The service manual says the damping mechanism does not suit the newer cartridges that track below its range, omits it from serial XA236534, and recommends disabling it on all XA tables (Factory, [XA service manual](../originals/xa-service-manual.pdf), pp. 1 and 4).
- **The arm begins to fail the McProud tracing test between ½ and ¾ g.** Villchur states it himself, with the cartridge mass as the variable (Factory, [Villchur 1962, Part 1](../originals/villchur-1962-part-1.pdf), p. 23). The filmed test passed it at 33 rpm with the stock shell; the tracking force used there was 1.96 g (Owner report, [owner tests](owner-tests.md)).
- **Arm resonance at 17 cps with the lab's cartridge.** United States Testing Company found the arm resonance at 17 cps, took it for the source of the main rumble component, and wrote that a lower resonance might be desirable (Period, [*High Fidelity*, March 1963](../originals/high-fidelity-1963-03-ar-report-pp59-60.pdf)). Villchur's own figure is the 10 cps region with typical cartridges of 1962 (Factory, Part 1, p. 22). The two stay side by side: the resonance depends on the cartridge.
- **Tracking error above the rated figure on the test unit.** Hirsch measured 0.4 to about 1 degree per inch against the rated 0.32. AR answered that the hand-assembled test unit was misaligned and that production jigs prevent it; the magazine printed both (Period, *HiFi/Stereo Review*, March 1963). No production table has been measured for it since.
- **The foam mat decomposed, and the motor often started backwards.** Both are named as early faults that were "soon corrected" (Period, *Audio*, July 1984). The permanent-magnet motor "is as willing to go clockwise as counterclockwise", which is why a starting motor was fitted (Factory, [Villchur 1962, Part 2](../originals/villchur-1962-part-2.pdf), p. 36).
- **The shell's studs strip and break.** "Threads in the cartridge mounting studs can be stripped, however, or the studs can be broken off, by excessive tightening of the screws" (Factory, [AR sheet L-1591](../originals/ar-cartridge-mount.jpg)). The shell has fixed studs, not slots: overhang is set by sliding the arm tube in its pivot with a tool and a template (Period, *HiFi/Stereo Review*, March 1963; Factory, Villchur 1962, Part 1, p. 69).
- **Speed is tied to the mains frequency.** The drive motor is synchronous, so a 60 Hz table runs slow on 50 Hz unless the 50-cycle pulley, larger in diameter, is fitted (Factory, XA service manual, p. 1). The same fact is what gives the exact speed: [period tests](period-tests.md).
- **Changing speed is slow.** Lift the mat and the outer platter and move the belt on the pulley, "rather lengthy for only one record or so" (Period, [*Audio*, April 1963](../originals/audio-1963-04-ar-profile-pp48-54.pdf)).
- **Speed falls under enough load.** The AR slows several tenths of a percent under sufficient added drag, through belt slip (Factory, Villchur 1962, Part 2, p. 38). It stayed inside 0.3 percent with 5 g added, the pass mark of his own test: [comparison A](compared.md#a-speed-under-added-load-one-method-one-tester).
- **Wow and flutter: not yet measured by the same method as the reference tables.** For the XA, the only factory figure is the NAB limit of 0.1 percent, a ceiling, not a reading. AR printed 0.03 percent in the XB1 manual, p. 2 (XB1 figure, same drive pattern), with the weighting not stated: see [the misread figure](README.md#a-figure-that-is-misread-01-percent-wow-and-flutter). The 1963 readings used instruments the magazines did not name. The open item is a measurement, not a weakness.

## Second family: The AR Turntable of 1983 and its relatives

All from the Boston Audio Society's measurements for *Audio*, July 1984 ([held](../archive/gamma-electronics/audio-1984-07-ar-turntable.md)), unless stated. Nothing here applies to the first family.

- **The arm's pivot is high.** The pivot is the point the arm swings on when it moves up and down. On the 1983 arm the lab measured it at nearly half an inch above the record (Period, *Audio*, July 1984). Over a warp, a high pivot slides the stylus forward and back in the groove, and the pitch wavers. The original XA arm has its pivot at record height for exactly this reason (Factory, Villchur 1962, Part 1, p. 22), so on this point the 1983 table is a step back from the XA.
- **Sound landing directly on the record is picked up about as much as on other tables.** The lab called its microphonic sensitivity about average (same review). This is not the same thing as vibration coming through the shelf: for that, the same review found 19 dB more margin before feedback than a Kenwood KD-500 with an SME arm. A heavier aftermarket mat improved the microphonics by about 6 dB but nearly bottomed the suspension.
- **Drive torque is low.** The speed dropped 0.27 percent under a 10 g load, and a Discwasher brush stops the platter (same review). The lab suggests a coin on the tail of a Dust Bug to cut its drag.
- **The cueing lever rocks the subchassis**, because it is mounted on the floating part, and the anti-skating moves the arm slightly outward when it is raised (same review).
- **The 3 Hz suspension needs a stable shelf.** Lateral or tipping motion from a poorly supported floor causes severe flutter or groove skipping (same review).
- **Its headshell is not plug-compatible** with the ADC-type shells it resembles (same review).
- **Wow and flutter measured 0.05 percent DIN weighted against 0.04 percent claimed** (same review; the claim is in the [1983 brochure](../archive/vinyl-nirvana/ar-turntable-history/ar-the-turntable-brochure.md)).
